# Air, DNA and the Next Generation: PM2.5, Genetics, Epigenetics and Cancer in Pakistan

**Tehreem Fatima**, Founder, Eco-Health Action Hub · Final-year BS Medical Laboratory Technology, NUMS · Interests: cancer genetics, epigenetics, translational medicine  
Eco-Health Action Hub · White paper, version 2.0 · 15 November 2026

---

## Summary

Fine particulate matter (PM2.5) is air pollution made of particles 2.5 micrometres (µm) across or smaller [1]. This paper explains, in plain language, what published research says PM2.5 does to our DNA, to cancer risk, and to pregnancy and the next generation. It also covers what can be done about it.

Three findings are well supported:

- Long-term exposure is linked to a higher risk of lung cancer, about 9% higher for each 10 µg/m³ (micrograms per cubic metre) [2]. This includes people who have never smoked [2,3].
- Pregnant women who breathe more polluted air are more likely to have babies born early or small [4,5].
- Soot particles from the air have been found in human placentas, cord blood and the organs of unborn babies [6,7].

Other claims are only suspected, or are unknown, and we say so. These include that PM2.5 changes the chemical "tags" on DNA in a lasting way, that it speeds up biological ageing, and that it affects grandchildren. This is educational information, not medical advice.

## 1. About this initiative

Eco-Health Action Hub is a student-founded initiative. It has three aims:

- **Translate the research** on air pollution, the human genome, epigenetics and cancer into plain language for families in Pakistan.
- **Publish open material:** referenced information and open air-quality data that anyone can check.
- **Build towards genomic and translational research** done under ethical approval. Section 8 describes this as a roadmap. It is not current work.

**How to read the evidence.** Each finding in this paper has one of three labels:

- **Known:** consistent evidence from large human studies or official evaluations.
- **Suspected:** some human evidence, but it is small, inconsistent or only shows an association, or the evidence comes mainly from animals or laboratory cells.
- **Unknown:** our review found no good source.

An *association* means two things occur together. It does not prove that one causes the other. For each finding we also say what kind of study it came from.

## 2. PM2.5 and how it reaches the body

The US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) defines PM2.5 as fine inhalable particles with diameters generally 2.5 µm and smaller. An average human hair is about 70 µm wide, about 30 times larger than the *largest* fine particle [1]. The EPA states that some small particles can get deep into the lungs and that some may get into the bloodstream [1]. The Health Effects Institute (HEI) adds that pollutants can enter the bloodstream directly through the lungs. It states that they are thought to cause oxidative stress and inflammation, which give rise to chronic disease [8].

In a small human study, volunteers inhaled engineered ultrafine gold particles. The gold was found in their blood and urine, and was still present three months later [9]. These were test particles, not outdoor PM2.5.

**Exposure in Pakistan.**
- The World Bank reports that the average exposure across Punjab's population is about 52 µg/m³ per year. Annual averages in central Lahore are 110–130 µg/m³ [10].
- The World Health Organization (WHO) annual guideline is 5 µg/m³ [11].
- In Punjab, about 23% of this pollution comes from homes, mostly from burning solid fuels for cooking, and 16% from transport [10].

## 3. Air and cancer

**Known.**
- **IARC classification.** The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), WHO's cancer agency, classifies outdoor air pollution and the particles in it as causing cancer in humans (Group 1). It found sufficient evidence for lung cancer [12,13].
- **Risk per 10 µg/m³.** A meta-analysis found that lung-cancer risk rose by about 9% for each 10 µg/m³ of long-term PM2.5 exposure (relative risk 1.09, 95% confidence interval 1.04–1.14) [2].

**People who have never smoked.**
- In the same meta-analysis, the estimate for never-smokers was 1.18 (1.00–1.39) [2]. The lower limit touches 1.00, so this evidence is moderate rather than strong.
- IARC researchers estimate that about 195,000 cases of adenocarcinoma, one common type of lung cancer, were linked to outdoor particle pollution worldwide in 2022 [3]. That is 114,486 in men and 80,378 in women [3].
- IARC describes "accumulating evidence of a causal link" between outdoor particle pollution and adenocarcinoma [14].

**How it might happen (suspected).**
- **Faulty cells in healthy lungs (human tissue).** Sequencing of normal-looking lung tissue found mutations in EGFR, a cancer-driver gene, in 18% of samples, and in KRAS in 53% [15]. In other words, many healthy people already carry a few faulty cells.
- **Inflammation helping those cells grow (mouse study).** In mice, pollution particles drew in immune cells (macrophages) that released a signal called IL-1β, and this inflammation helped already-mutated lung cells grow into early tumours [15].
- **Human populations.** In four cohorts covering 32,957 cases, higher PM2.5 levels were associated with more EGFR-driven lung cancer [15].
- **Tumour genomes in never-smokers.** The Sherlock-Lung study sequenced the whole genomes of tumours from 871 never-smokers. Tumours from regions with higher PM2.5 carried more mutations, more TP53 mutations and shorter telomeres [16]. Exposure was estimated by region, not measured for each person, so the study cannot show that pollution caused any one person's cancer [16].

**Unknown.** Our review found no sources good enough to say whether PM2.5 causes breast, bladder or liver cancer, or childhood leukaemia. This paper makes no claims about them.

## 4. Your DNA and the air

**4.1 DNA damage (suspected for the general public).**
- **Comet assay meta-analysis.** The comet assay is a laboratory test that measures breaks in DNA. People exposed to combustion pollution showed more DNA breaks in their blood cells than comparison groups:
  - traffic exhaust: standardised mean difference 0.62 (0.36–0.89), from 21 studies;
  - wood, dung or crop-waste smoke: 1.73 (0.72–2.74), from 10 studies [17].

  The authors note possible confounding and measurement bias [17].
- **Micronuclei.** People exposed to particles also showed more micronuclei, small broken pieces of chromosomes [18].

**Studies from Pakistan:**
- **Karak.** Traffic conductors had an average comet tail length of 28.69 µm, against 8.62 µm in comparison people. Coal miners had 30.16 µm, against 9.82 µm [19]. PM2.5 was not measured for each person.
- **Traffic police wardens.** 28% of their cells were damaged, against 3.6% in comparison people. The authors linked this to cadmium and zinc in the blood, not directly to PM2.5 [20].
- **Lahore laboratory study.** In laboratory tests, PM2.5 collected in Lahore increased markers of DNA and chromosome damage in cells [21]. This does not show that the same happens inside the body.

These studies are small and mostly cover workers, so they should not be applied to whole cities.

**4.2 Signs linked to ageing (suspected).**
- **Telomeres.** These are the "end caps" on chromosomes, a sign linked to ageing [22]. In 433,535 UK adults, air pollution was associated with telomere length in white blood cells, and the relationship was not a straight line [22].
- **Mitochondrial DNA.** In a small controlled experiment, two hours of breathing PM2.5 lowered mitochondrial DNA content by 11.1% (95% CI 0.4–21.7%) [23]. Mitochondria are the cell's "power stations".

**4.3 Epigenetics: the chemical "tags" on DNA (suspected, not proven).**
The best-studied epigenetic change is DNA methylation, the chemical "tags" attached to DNA [23,24].

- **Short-term experiment.** In a small trial, breathing PM2.5 for two hours changed DNA methylation measured in blood [23]. Whether this harms health is unknown.
- **Overall methylation.** Reviews find inconsistent links between PM2.5 and overall DNA methylation, with few findings repeated in independent groups [25–27]. Most studies came from countries with low pollution levels [27].
- **"Epigenetic clocks"** estimate the body's biological age from methylation patterns [28]. The studies disagree:

| Study | Who | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| Taiwan Biobank [29] | 2,462 adults | Faster epigenetic ageing with more pollution |
| KORA, Germany [30] | 1,651 adults | Faster epigenetic ageing with more pollution |
| Systematic review [31] | 19 air-pollution studies, counted not pooled | 15 (79%) found at least one significant positive link, and 4 (21%) at least one negative link |
| 2026 meta-analysis [28] | 18 studies, 363,381 people, pooled | No statistically significant link |
| US children [32] | 457 children | Effect in the opposite direction, but not significant after correcting for multiple tests |

**4.4 Genes that may change how sensitive we are (suspected).**
- **The genes studied.** Some people inherit inactive ("null") versions of genes called GSTM1 and GSTT1 [33].
- **Most studies find an effect.** In one review, 15 of 22 studies found that these genes changed the link between indoor air pollution and asthma or lung function [33]. A 2026 review of 48 studies on outdoor air pollution found 89 gene variants, in 53 genes, that significantly changed its effects on breathing health [34].
- **Not all studies agree.** A 2023 review concluded that the role of the GSTM1-null type is not yet clear [35].
- **Gas cooking.** In a European study, gas cooking and the GSTM1-null type interacted in their effect on airway sensitivity [36].
- **Genes and pollution together.** In UK Biobank, inherited genetic risk and air pollution together were associated with shorter telomeres [22].

## 5. Mothers, babies and the next generation

**Known.**
- **US systematic review.** It covered 68 studies and about 32.8 million births. Of the 58 studies on air pollutants, 48 (84%) found significant links with preterm birth, low birth weight or stillbirth [4].
- **Earlier meta-analysis.** It found small but significant associations with preterm birth and low birth weight [37].

**Global burden (modelled estimates).**
- In 2019, 15.6% of all low-birth-weight births and 35.7% of all preterm births worldwide were attributed to outdoor and indoor PM2.5 combined. That is about 2.76 million babies born too small and 5.87 million born too early [5].
- South Asia, which includes Pakistan, had the highest burden of any region [5].

**Particles reach the unborn baby (known that they are present; unknown what they do).**
- **Placenta.** Soot (black carbon) particles were found in human placentas, and the amount was linked to the soot level where the mother lived [6]. Experts later debated exactly which side of the placenta the particles were on [38,39].
- **Cord blood and fetal organs.** A second study looked at 60 mothers and their newborns, and at tissue from fetuses in early pregnancy. Soot was found in every sample of maternal blood, placenta and cord blood, and in fetal liver, lung and brain tissue [7]. Cord-blood levels rose with the mother's home exposure (r = 0.68) [7].
- **Health effect.** What these particles do to the baby is not yet known [7].

**At birth (suspected).**
- **Methylation in newborns.** Babies whose mothers breathed more PM2.5 showed small differences in methylation at 14 sites on their DNA, in a meta-analysis of 9 birth cohorts [24]. Whether this affects their health is unknown.
- **Shorter telomeres (Belgium).** Each 5 µg/m³ more PM2.5 during pregnancy was associated with telomeres 8.8% shorter in cord blood and 13.2% shorter in the placenta [40].
- **Not confirmed in Europe.** In 1,396 European children, the estimate for PM2.5 during pregnancy was not statistically significant [41].

**Grandchildren and fathers (unknown).**
- **Grandchildren.** Our review found no source showing that air pollution affects grandchildren through inherited epigenetic changes, so we make no such claim.
- **Fathers.** We found no approved source on fathers' exposure and sperm.

## 6. What can be done: four levels

Evidence labels follow those in this project's evidence table.

**Individual.**
- **Check the Air Quality Index (AQI)** and follow its official activity advice, which gets stricter as the category rises [42–44].
  The website uses the US EPA breakpoints for 24-hour PM2.5, in µg/m³: Good 0.0–9.0, Moderate 9.1–35.4, Unhealthy for Sensitive Groups 35.5–55.4, Unhealthy 55.5–125.4, Very Unhealthy 125.5–225.4, and Hazardous 225.5 and above [42].
- **If you wear a mask, make sure it fits.** In a laboratory test, total leakage around worn masks ranged from 3% to 68% [45]. In trials in Beijing, wearing a facemask improved short-term blood pressure and heart measures [46,47]. *Label: moderate.*
- **In pregnancy,** we found no approved source on mask use, so ask your doctor or midwife.

**Household.**
- **HEPA air cleaners.** These lower indoor PM2.5 and slightly lower blood pressure [48,49]. WHO Europe advises choosing one sized for the room [50]. *Label: moderate for reducing exposure.*
- **HEPA in pregnancy.** In a trial of 540 pregnant women in Mongolia, purifiers cut indoor PM2.5 by 29% [51]. Average birth weight was 3,550 g with a purifier and 3,450 g without, a difference that was not statistically clear. Babies born at full term were about 85 g heavier [51]. The original results also showed a higher chance of preterm birth in the purifier group (odds ratio 2.3), but a later analysis found that this estimate moved towards no effect after correcting for a statistical bias [52]. *Label: limited for birth outcomes.*
- **Cleaner cooking.** In a trial of 3,200 pregnant women in four countries, switching to gas (LPG) stoves cut smoke exposure a lot. Babies were on average only 19.6 g heavier, which was not significant [53]. *Label: strong evidence that this single step did not raise average birth weight.*
- **What these trials mean.** Lowering exposure lowers what you breathe, but no single household step has yet been shown to improve birth weight clearly [51,53].
- **Windows.** In one study, indoor PM2.5 was higher with windows open than closed [54]. *Label: limited.*
- **Diet and vitamins.**
  - In a tiny trial, B vitamins reduced some laboratory-measured changes after breathing PM2.5 [23]. Other scientists replied that the picture is still incomplete [55].
  - In one US study, the link between PM2.5 in mid-pregnancy and shorter newborn telomeres was significant only in boys whose mothers had low antioxidant intake [56].

  *Label: very limited.* This project does not recommend supplements for protection against pollution. In pregnancy, follow your clinician's advice on vitamins.

**Community and workplace.** The Pakistani studies found more DNA damage in traffic workers and coal miners than in comparison groups [19,20]. Workers like these may be worth including in protection programmes and research. *Label: limited.*

**Policy.**
- **China.** Between 2013 and 2017, population-weighted PM2.5 fell from 61.8 to 42.0 µg/m³, mostly because of emission controls. Modelling estimates that the main measures prevented about 0.37 million deaths [57].
- **Pakistan.** The 2023 National Clean Air Policy lists measures including cleaner fuel standards and preventing crop-residue burning [58].
- **Not yet known.** We found no published study showing that cleaner air reverses DNA or epigenetic changes.

## 7. A laboratory perspective

The tests behind this research are familiar laboratory methods:

- **Comet assay.** In a review of 81 studies, 66 (81.5%) found significantly more DNA damage in exposed groups than in comparison groups [59].
- **Telomere length.** In the Belgian newborn study, this was measured by quantitative PCR in cord blood and placenta [40].

These are research tools for comparing groups of people. Our review found no official source supporting their use to diagnose harm from pollution in an individual. This initiative therefore does not offer or recommend such tests.

## 8. Vision: from awareness to genomic and translational research

*This section describes future aims, not current activities.*

**The exposome.** This is everything a person is exposed to over a lifetime, starting before birth, studied alongside the genome [60]. Large studies already combine DNA and air-pollution data:

- Sherlock-Lung, on lung cancer in never-smokers [16]
- the PACE consortium of birth cohorts [24]
- UK Biobank [22]

Our review found no Pakistani cohort that has analysed air pollution alongside genomic data.

**Pakistan's genomic resources.**
- **PROMIS** (Pakistan Risk of Myocardial Infarction Study) has collected genetic and health data from thousands of people with heart attacks and comparison groups [61]. It has also found many people carrying two non-working copies of certain genes, which is common where cousin marriage is common [62].
- **The Pakistan Genome Resource** has analysed exomes and genomes from 173,303 people [63].

**Governance.** Pakistan's National Bioethics Committee issued national guidelines on human biological materials in 2020, but experts describe gaps in ethics, quality standards and data governance for biobanks [64].

**Our roadmap:**

1. **Now.** Plain-language awareness, open exposure data and an open evidence table.
2. **Next.** Partnerships with ethics-approved research groups, to bring Pakistani exposure data into genomic and epigenomic studies of cancer and pregnancy.
3. **Always.** Informed consent, data protection and community benefit come before any data collection. This initiative will not collect samples or genetic data itself without ethical approval and appropriate governance.

## 9. Limitations

- **Not a systematic review.** The evidence table was compiled with AI-assisted searches and approved by the author. Several sources were read only as abstracts.
- **Estimated exposure.** Most human studies estimate exposure from home address or region rather than measuring it for each person.
- **Lab tests are research tools.** The DNA, telomere and methylation measures are not clinical tests for individuals.
- **Evidence from elsewhere.** Little of the genetic and epigenetic research comes from Pakistan. The Pakistani studies are small, focus on workers or were done in the laboratory.
- **No local measurements.** Nothing biological was measured at the project's monitoring locations, and no such claims are made.
- **Missing details.** Some references are still missing author or year details. They are marked.
- **The author** is a laboratory technology student, not a physician or epidemiologist.
