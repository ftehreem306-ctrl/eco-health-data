# PM2.5 Air Pollution and Health in Pakistan: A Referenced Overview

**Tehreem Fatima**, BS Medical Laboratory Technology (final year), NUMS, Pakistan
Eco-Health Action Hub · 13 November 2026

---

## Summary

This paper summarises published evidence on fine particulate matter (PM2.5) and health, focusing on Pakistan. Meta-analyses of cohort studies link long-term PM2.5 exposure with higher mortality [15,16]. The International Agency for Research on Cancer classifies particulate matter in outdoor air as carcinogenic to humans [18]. Modelled estimates attribute more than 200,000 deaths in Pakistan in 2023 to air pollution [13]. A satellite-based estimate puts Pakistan's average PM2.5 in 2023 at about 39 µg/m³ [6], against a World Health Organization (WHO) annual guideline of 5 µg/m³ [3]. Randomised trials of portable HEPA air cleaners [28,29] and of facemasks [32,33] have measured short-term outcomes such as blood pressure. The evidence for diet and supplements is limited [37]. Human studies of PM2.5 and DNA methylation are inconsistent, and none has shown that these changes cause disease [21–23]. This paper is educational information, not medical advice.

## 1. Background

### 1.1 What PM2.5 is

The United States Environmental Protection Agency (US EPA) defines PM2.5 as fine inhalable particles with diameters generally 2.5 µm (micrometres) and smaller [1]. The size definition refers to *aerodynamic* diameter [2]. The US EPA gives the width of an average human hair as about 70 µm, which makes a hair about 30 times larger than the *largest* fine particle [1]. Because 2.5 µm is the upper limit, the "30 times" comparison applies only to the largest particles in the PM2.5 range [1].

### 1.2 Guideline values and national standards

The WHO 2021 Air Quality Guidelines set an annual PM2.5 guideline of 5 µg/m³ (micrograms per cubic metre of air), with interim targets of 35, 25, 15 and 10 µg/m³ [3]. The 24-hour guideline is 15 µg/m³, with interim targets of 75, 50, 37.5 and 25 µg/m³. The 24-hour guideline is defined as the 99th percentile of daily values, which allows 3–4 days above it per year [3].

Official sources disagree about Pakistan's national standard:

- A 2024 Pakistan Environmental Protection Agency (Pak-EPA) document states a 24-hour limit of 35 µg/m³ and an annual limit of 15 µg/m³ [4]. These revised limits took effect from 1 January 2013 [5].
- The Air Quality Life Index (AQLI), a University of Chicago institute, states that Pakistan revised the annual standard from 15 to 35 µg/m³ in 2023 [6].
- The 2023 National Clean Air Policy lists ambient PM2.5 *targets* of 35 µg/m³ (annual) and 75 µg/m³ (24-hour) [7]. These match the WHO first interim targets [3,7].

The original Gazette notification was not opened during the evidence review, so it could not be confirmed which value is currently in force.

Punjab notified its own Environmental Quality Standards for Ambient Air in 2016 [9]. The World Bank describes Punjab's annual standard as 15 µg/m³ [8].

### 1.3 The scale used for risk categories

The risk categories in this project follow the US EPA Air Quality Index (AQI). In May 2024 the US annual PM2.5 standard was lowered from 12.0 to 9.0 µg/m³, and the 24-hour standard of 35 µg/m³ was kept [10]. The current AQI breakpoints for PM2.5 are defined for 24-hour averages [11]:

| AQI category | PM2.5, 24-hour average (µg/m³) | AQI value |
|---|---|---|
| Good | 0.0–9.0 | 0–50 |
| Moderate | 9.1–35.4 | 51–100 |
| Unhealthy for Sensitive Groups | 35.5–55.4 | 101–150 |
| Unhealthy | 55.5–125.4 | 151–200 |
| Very Unhealthy | 125.5–225.4 | 201–300 |
| Hazardous | 225.5 and above | 301+ |

Sources: [10,11].

Before 2024, the scale was different, so older charts will not match [10]:

- "Good" ran from 0 to 12.0 µg/m³.
- "Very Unhealthy" ran from 150.5 to 250.4 µg/m³.
- "Hazardous" had two bands, which have since been merged into one.

Each category has official activity advice. The groups the advice treats as sensitive are people with lung disease such as asthma, older adults, children and teenagers, and people who are active outdoors [12]. The evidence review found no official Punjab EPA document giving concentration breakpoints for its own index, so this project uses the US scale.

## 2. What PM2.5 does in the body

### 2.1 How far particles travel

The US EPA states that some particles smaller than 10 µm can get deep into the lungs and some may get into the bloodstream. It identifies particles smaller than 2.5 µm as posing the greatest risk to health [1]. The Health Effects Institute (HEI) states that pollutants can enter the bloodstream directly through the lungs [13].

Direct human evidence is limited. In one study, 14 healthy volunteers inhaled engineered gold nanoparticles [14]:

- Gold was detected in their blood and urine from 15 minutes to 24 hours after inhalation.
- Gold was still present three months later.
- In 12 surgical patients, gold was found in plaque removed from the carotid (neck) arteries.

In mice, 5-nanometre gold particles collected preferentially in inflamed lesions in blood vessels [14]. These were engineered *ultrafine* test particles, not outdoor PM2.5. The study is small, so its evidence is limited [14].

### 2.2 Proposed mechanisms

The HEI states that air pollution is thought to cause oxidative stress and inflammation, which give rise to chronic disease. It also lists pathways through the nervous and hormonal (endocrine) systems [13]. The HEI presents these as likely mechanisms ("is thought to"), not as proven causal steps.

### 2.3 Long-term exposure and death

The systematic review that WHO commissioned for its 2021 guidelines pooled 25 cohort studies, mostly from high-income countries [15]. Each 10 µg/m³ increase in long-term PM2.5 was associated with a higher risk of death from natural causes: relative risk (RR) 1.08, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.06–1.09. The results varied considerably between studies (I² = 88.9%) [15].

An updated review included 106 studies. From 53 of them, it estimated an RR for all-cause death of 1.095 (95% CI 1.064–1.127) per 10 µg/m³ [16]. Every exposure–outcome pair it analysed showed a positive association, except PM10 and death from stroke [16].

The two reviews give similar results for specific causes of death, each per 10 µg/m³ [15,16]:

| Cause of death | Original review [15] | Updated review [16] |
|---|---|---|
| Ischaemic heart disease | RR 1.16 (1.10–1.21) | RR 1.143 (1.102–1.186) |
| Stroke | RR 1.11 (1.04–1.18) | RR 1.146 (1.101–1.192) |
| Acute lower respiratory infection | RR 1.16 (1.01–1.34), from 4 studies | RR 1.204 (1.095–1.325), from 12 studies |

The updated review rated its certainty for stroke as moderate [16]. All of these are pooled associations from observational cohort studies.

### 2.4 Short-term exposure

A WHO-commissioned review of daily (time-series) studies found that each 10 µg/m³ rise in 24-hour PM2.5 was associated with about a 0.65% increase in deaths from all causes: RR 1.0065, 95% CI 1.0044–1.0086 [17]. It also found positive associations with deaths from cardiovascular, respiratory and cerebrovascular causes [17].

### 2.5 Cancer

The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) classified both outdoor air pollution and the particulate matter in it as carcinogenic to humans (Group 1). It found sufficient evidence for lung cancer and noted a positive association with bladder cancer [18,19]. IARC cited increased genetic damage in exposed humans and experimental animals as strong mechanistic support [18].

### 2.6 Other conditions

The HEI's State of Global Air 2025 report uses Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2023 modelling. It links air pollution to the following share of deaths from each condition [13]:

| Condition | Share of deaths linked to air pollution |
|---|---|
| COPD (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease) | about 1 in 2 |
| Heart disease | about 1 in 4 |
| Diabetes | nearly 1 in 6 |
| Dementia | more than 1 in 4 (626,000 deaths in 2023) |

These figures cover *all* air pollution, not PM2.5 alone, and they are model estimates [13]. The same report associates air pollution with asthma flare-ups, faster decline in lung function and impaired lung development in children [13]. It is a narrative review, so this evidence is limited.

### 2.7 Pregnancy

A meta-analysis of 23 observational studies looked at exposure over the whole pregnancy [20]:

- Per interquartile-range increase in PM2.5, the odds ratio (OR) for preterm birth was 1.03 (95% CI 1.01–1.05).
- For low birth weight in full-term babies, the OR was 1.03 (95% CI 1.02–1.03).
- Neither association was significant when expressed per 10 µg/m³.

The effects are small and the studies varied, so the evidence is moderate [20].

### 2.8 Epigenetics: what is and is not known

Three systematic reviews describe the human evidence on PM2.5 and DNA methylation:

- **2021 meta-analysis of 38 articles:** the pooled association between PM2.5 and overall (global) DNA methylation was inverse but imprecise, and was based on only five studies. Epigenome-wide studies reported 201 CpG sites and 148 regions, but only one location (NXN) appeared in two studies [21].
- **2026 review of 52 articles** (30 on outdoor and 22 on workplace exposure): long-term exposure showed no consistent association with global DNA methylation. Hypermethylation of the gene SOX2 came from only two studies, and epigenome-wide studies reported up to 189 locations, few of which were replicated in independent groups [22].
- **2018 review of 82 manuscripts:** 85% were observational, 66% were in adults and 79% studied DNA methylation. Several studies linked particulate matter with lower global or LINE-1 methylation and with faster ageing on DNA-methylation "clocks", but most effects in observational studies were modest [23].

None of these reviews shows that methylation changes cause PM2.5-related disease. The findings are small, vary between studies and are poorly replicated [21–23]. No epigenetic or clinical effects have been measured at this project's monitoring locations, and the project makes no such claims.

## 3. The burden in Pakistan

### 3.1 Deaths

The HEI estimates that air pollution caused 7.9 million deaths worldwide in 2023 (95% uncertainty interval 6.4–9.4 million), about 1 in 8 of all deaths. Of these, 4.9 million were attributed to outdoor PM2.5. Air pollution was the second-leading risk factor for death, after high blood pressure [13].

Estimates for Pakistan, all from modelling:

- **2023:** more than 200,000 deaths from all air pollution (outdoor PM2.5, household air pollution and ozone). The HEI reports this as a threshold, not an exact national figure [13].
- **2021:** 256,000 deaths, from the HEI's previous report. That report used an earlier GBD cycle, so it is not directly comparable with the 2023 figure [24].
- **2019:** about 230,000 premature deaths from outdoor and household PM2.5 combined, estimated by the World Bank [8].

### 3.2 Exposure

The estimates differ. They cover different areas and periods, and some use satellite data while others use monitoring:

- **AQLI, satellite-based, 2023** [6]:
  - National population-weighted average of about 39 µg/m³, and an estimated 3.3 years of life expectancy lost compared with the WHO guideline.
  - Lahore division about 64.03 µg/m³ (5.8 years lost), Peshawar 60.21, Islamabad 51.15 and Karachi 32.46 µg/m³.
  - Dust and sea salt are removed, and a loss of 0.98 years of life expectancy per 10 µg/m³ is assumed.
  - Average concentrations rose by 55.7% between 1998 and 2023.
- **Satellite and reanalysis study:** a mean annual PM2.5 over Pakistan of 54.7 µg/m³ for 2003–2020 [25].
- **World Bank** [8]:
  - Punjab's population-weighted annual average is about 52 µg/m³.
  - Annual averages in central Lahore were 110–130 µg/m³.
  - 24-hour peaks exceeded 300–400 µg/m³ during acute episodes.

### 3.3 Sources and smog

World Bank modelling attributes population-weighted PM2.5 in Punjab to the following sources [8]:

| Source | Share of PM2.5 |
|---|---|
| Natural sources | about 25% |
| Household cooking with solid fuels | 23% |
| Transport | 16% |
| Industry, including brick kilns | 16% |
| Agriculture, including crop-residue burning | 13% |
| Burning of municipal waste | 7% |

Lahore has about 7.4 million vehicles, and two-wheelers make up 82% of registrations [8]. The HEI attributes smog episodes in South Asia largely to agricultural burns and the burning of fossil fuels [13]. No source on the seasonal timing or weather causes of Punjab's smog was found in the evidence review, so this paper does not describe them.

### 3.4 Economic cost

The World Bank has published three estimates, using different methods:

- 2.5–6.5% of Pakistan's 2016 gross domestic product (GDP), with outdoor air pollution about 2% and household air pollution about 2.5% [26]
- a loss of 6.5% of GDP per year [27]
- health costs from PM2.5 equal to 9% of GDP in 2019 [8]

## 4. What individuals can do

The evidence labels follow the strength ratings in this project's evidence table:

- **Strong:** WHO, IARC or other official evaluations, or a large, consistent meta-analysis.
- **Moderate:** a meta-analysis with varied results, authoritative modelling, or a few randomised trials.
- **Limited:** small, single, observational or secondary sources.

**Portable HEPA air cleaners: moderate.**
- A meta-analysis of 17 trials (about 880 participants) compared real filtration with sham filtration [28]:
  - Indoor PM2.5 fell by 59.8% on average (range 23–82%).
  - Systolic blood pressure fell by 2.35 mmHg (95% CI −4.5 to −0.2).
  - The change in diastolic blood pressure was not significant.
- In a one-year randomised trial in about 50 older adults in Hong Kong, indoor PM2.5 was about 28% lower and diastolic blood pressure fell significantly [29].
- WHO Europe advises using HEPA portable air cleaners with a clean air delivery rate suited to the room size [30].

**Well-fitted facemasks and respirators: moderate.**
- A laboratory study tested nine masks sold in Beijing on ten volunteers [31]:
  - Between 0.26% and 29% of particles passed through the filter material.
  - When the masks were worn, total inward leakage was 3–68% while sitting and 7–66% while active.
  - Only one mask averaged under 10% leakage in both conditions.
- In a randomised crossover trial, 15 healthy young adults in Beijing had a systolic blood pressure of 114 mmHg with a mask and 121 mmHg without, during a two-hour city walk [32]. This trial is small, so its evidence is limited.
- In a randomised crossover trial in 98 patients with coronary heart disease, mean arterial pressure was 93 mmHg with the mask and 96 mmHg without. Heart-rate variability and ECG signs of ischaemia also improved [33].
- These trials measured short-term outcomes, not heart attacks or deaths [33].
- The evidence review found no primary source for the filtration percentages of N95, KN95, FFP2, surgical or cloth masks, so none are given.

**Checking air quality and reducing outdoor exertion: moderate.** Official AQI advice for PM2.5 gets stricter as the category rises [12]:

| AQI category | Sensitive groups | Everyone else |
|---|---|---|
| Unhealthy for Sensitive Groups | Make outdoor activity shorter and less intense | — |
| Unhealthy | Avoid long or intense outdoor activity | Reduce long or intense outdoor activity |
| Very Unhealthy | Avoid all outdoor physical activity | Avoid long or intense outdoor activity |
| Hazardous | Remain indoors and keep activity levels low | — |

A European Respiratory Society review grades personal strategies by strength of evidence [34]. These include checking forecasts, staying indoors, using air cleaners and wearing masks, and choosing low-pollution travel routes is graded C [34]. WHO Europe also advises exercising away from pollution sources [30].

**Keeping windows closed during pollution peaks: limited.** A review of household measures reports a study in which indoor PM2.5, blood pressure and heart rate were higher with windows open than with windows closed [35].

**Do-it-yourself box-fan filters (Corsi-Rosenthal boxes): limited.** In a laboratory chamber, a box fan with five MERV-13 filters reached an effective clean air delivery rate of about 600–850 cubic feet per minute, depending on fan speed [36]. Particles were counted with an instrument whose median particle size was about 1.2 µm, so the result is not a measure of PM2.5 mass [36]. No health studies were found.

**Not burning household waste: moderate evidence of contribution.** Municipal waste burning accounts for about 7% of population-weighted PM2.5 in Punjab [8]. No study of the personal health benefit of not burning waste was found. An earlier claim that waste burning is "hundreds of times more hazardous than traffic" had no source and has been removed.

**Diet and supplements: limited.** A review in a European Respiratory Society journal graded the evidence for every dietary and supplement category as C, and found that the evidence for omega-3 and N-acetylcysteine was mostly observational [37]. This project does not recommend supplements as protection against PM2.5.

## 5. What policy can do

**London's Ultra Low Emission Zone (ULEZ).** The three evaluations disagree, mainly because they use different methods [38–40]:

- The Greater London Authority estimated that the zone reduced roadside nitrogen dioxide (NO2) by about 29 µg/m³. That is 37% lower than a modelled scenario without the zone [38].
- An independent causal analysis found that the ULEZ reduced NO2 by less than 3% on average, with no significant effect on ozone or PM2.5 [39].
- A 2025 study found that NOx fell by 28.8% at central London traffic sites three months after the first phase. It found no detectable effect on NO2 from the 2023 London-wide expansion [40].

All three mainly concern NO2 and NOx, not PM2.5.

**China's 2013 Action Plan.**
- Between 2013 and 2017, China's population-weighted PM2.5 fell from 61.8 to 42.0 µg/m³, about 32%. Modelling estimated that the main measures prevented about 0.37 million deaths [41].
- Studies summarised by the HEI found three results in areas covered by a clean-heating programme in northern China [13]:
  - Outdoor PM2.5 fell by 2.4–7 µg/m³ more than elsewhere.
  - Heart attacks fell by 6.6% in Beijing townships with high coverage.
  - Blood pressure fell by 1–2 mmHg.
- No published evaluation of Beijing's heavy-pollution alert system was found.

**Pakistan.** The 2023 National Clean Air Policy lists actions including Euro-5/6 fuel standards, enforcement of industrial emission limits and prevention of crop-residue burning [7]. No published evaluation of the policy, of Punjab's smog-season measures or of crop-burning bans was found.

## 6. Limitations of this project

- **Not a systematic review.** The evidence table was compiled with AI-assisted web searches and approved by the author. Several sources were read only as abstracts or official summaries.
- **Modelled figures.** Death, exposure and cost figures come from models with uncertainty ranges. Figures from different reports and years are not directly comparable.
- **Evidence from other countries.** The pooled mortality estimates rely mostly on cohorts from high-income countries [15].
- **Unresolved national standard.** The legal status of Pakistan's PM2.5 standard could not be confirmed.
- **No local health measurements.** No health, clinical or epigenetic outcomes were measured at this project's monitoring locations.
- **Incomplete references.** Several references below have author or date details still to be confirmed in the project's final audit (Phase 5). They are marked.
- **The author is a laboratory technology student,** not a physician or epidemiologist.
