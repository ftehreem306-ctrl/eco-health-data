At a glance: what science says

PM2.5 means tiny air particles 2.5 micrometres across or smaller. The largest of them is about 30 times thinner than a human hair [1]. Some may reach the bloodstream [1,8].

Known

  • Polluted air causes lung cancer (WHO's cancer agency, Group 1) [12,13].
  • Lung-cancer risk rises about 9% for each 10 µg/m³ more PM2.5 over the long term [2].
  • Mothers who breathe more polluted air are more likely to have babies born early or small [4,5].
  • Soot particles from the air have been found in placentas, cord blood and the organs of unborn babies [6,7].

Suspected

  • Pollution helps lung cells that are already faulty grow into tumours (shown in mice) [15].
  • Heavily exposed workers show more DNA damage in blood tests [17,19].
  • Pollution changes chemical "tags" (methylation) on DNA in adults and newborns [23,24].
  • Some inherited gene types make people more sensitive [33,34].

Unknown

  • What the particles found in the placenta do to the baby [7].
  • Whether pollution makes the body's biological clock run faster: pooled studies could not confirm this [28].
  • Whether grandchildren are affected: our review found no source.
  • Whether cleaner air reverses DNA changes: our review found no source.

Air and cancer

  • Lung cancer. Long-term PM2.5 exposure is linked to higher lung-cancer risk: about 9% higher per 10 µg/m³ (relative risk 1.09) [2]. Known
  • People who have never smoked. They are affected too. The estimate for never-smokers was 1.18, with a lower limit of 1.00 [2]. Scientists at WHO's cancer agency estimate that about 195,000 cases of one common type of lung cancer (adenocarcinoma) worldwide in 2022 were linked to outdoor particle pollution [3,14]. Moderate
  • How it may happen.
    • Many healthy lungs already contain a few cells with cancer-driver mutations, in genes such as EGFR and KRAS [15].
    • In mice, particles caused inflammation that helped these cells grow [15].
    • In never-smokers, lung tumours from more polluted regions carried more genetic changes [16].
    These studies cannot show what caused any one person's cancer [16]. Suspected
  • Other cancers. Our review found no strong source for breast, bladder or liver cancer, or childhood leukaemia, so we make no claim.

Your DNA and the air

Breaks in DNA

Moderate People heavily exposed to smoke from traffic, or from wood, dung or crop-waste fires, show more DNA breaks in their blood cells [17]. They also show more small broken pieces of chromosome [18].

Studies from Pakistan

Limited Traffic conductors and coal miners in Karak [19], and traffic wardens [20], had more DNA damage than comparison groups. In laboratory tests, Lahore PM2.5 increased DNA-damage markers in cells [21]. These studies are small and do not apply to whole cities.

Chemical "tags" on DNA (epigenetics)

Suspected In a small experiment, breathing PM2.5 for two hours changed DNA methylation measured in blood [23]. Reviews find inconsistent results [25,26,27].

"Biological age"

Unclear Single studies in Taiwan and Germany linked pollution to an older "epigenetic age" [29,30]. A 2026 meta-analysis of 18 studies found no significant link [28]. A children's study pointed the opposite way, but not after statistical correction [32].

Chromosome "end caps"

Suspected In 433,535 UK adults, air pollution was associated with telomere length, a sign linked to ageing. Inherited genetic risk added to the effect [22].

Genes that change sensitivity

Suspected People with "null" versions of the GSTM1 or GSTT1 genes were often more affected by polluted air [33]. A 2026 review found 89 gene variants that changed pollution's effects on breathing health [34]. Their role is not yet clear [35].

Mothers, babies and the next generation

  • Birth outcomes. Across US studies of about 32.8 million births, 48 of 58 air-pollution studies found links between air pollution and babies born early, small or stillborn [4]. Known
  • The global picture. In 2019, outdoor and indoor PM2.5 together were estimated to contribute to about 5.87 million preterm births and 2.76 million low-birth-weight births worldwide. South Asia had the highest burden [5]. Modelled
  • Particles reach the unborn baby. Soot was found in every sample of maternal blood, placenta and cord blood, and in fetal liver, lung and brain tissue [7]. What it does there is not known. Present; effect unknown
  • Newborn DNA.
    • Babies of mothers exposed to more PM2.5 had small methylation differences at a few DNA sites [24].
    • In Belgium, they also had shorter telomeres in cord blood and placenta [40]. In a larger European study, the estimate for exposure during pregnancy was not statistically significant [41].
    Suspected
  • Grandchildren and fathers. Our review found no source on effects passed to grandchildren or through fathers' sperm, so we make no claim.

What you can do: four levels

1. You

  • Check the category in the readings below and follow its advice [42,43,44]. Moderate
  • If you wear a mask, make sure it fits. Leakage around worn masks ranged from 3% to 68% [45]. In Beijing trials, masks improved short-term blood pressure and heart measures [46,47]. Moderate
  • If you are pregnant, ask your doctor or midwife about masks: we found no source specific to pregnancy.

2. Your home

  • A HEPA air cleaner sized for the room lowers indoor PM2.5 [48,49,50]. Moderate
  • In pregnancy, purifiers cut indoor PM2.5 by 29%, but the effect on birth weight was not clear [51,52]. Limited
  • Switching from wood or dung to gas cooking cut smoke a lot, but did not significantly raise birth weight [53]. Lower exposure is still lower exposure.
  • Keep windows closed during pollution peaks [54]. Limited
  • Supplements are not proven to protect against pollution [23,55,56]. In pregnancy, follow your clinician's advice on vitamins. Very limited

3. Community and workplace

  • Workers with heavy exposure, such as traffic workers and coal miners, showed more DNA damage [19,20]. They may deserve priority in protection and research. Limited

4. Policy

  • Between 2013 and 2017, China's emission controls cut population-weighted PM2.5 from 61.8 to 42.0 µg/m³ [57].
  • Pakistan's 2023 National Clean Air Policy lists measures such as cleaner fuels and preventing crop burning [58].
  • In Punjab, about 23% of PM2.5 comes from homes, mostly solid-fuel cooking [10].

Vision: from awareness to genomic and translational research

This section describes future aims, not current activities.

Scientists use the word exposome for everything a person is exposed to over a lifetime, studied alongside the genome [60]. Large studies abroad already combine DNA and air-pollution data [16,22,24]. Our review found no Pakistani study that has done this yet.

Pakistan already has major genetic resources, including PROMIS [61,62] and the Pakistan Genome Resource, with 173,303 people [63]. National guidelines on human biological materials exist (2020), but experts describe gaps in biobank ethics, standards and data governance [64].

  1. Now: plain-language awareness, open exposure data and an open evidence table.
  2. Next: partnerships with ethics-approved research groups to link Pakistani exposure data with genomic and epigenomic studies of cancer and pregnancy.
  3. Always: consent, data protection and community benefit first. This initiative will not collect samples or genetic data without ethical approval.

Methods and data

  • Evidence. Every claim comes from an evidence table approved by the author. Findings are labelled known, suspected or unknown, with the type of study. The full text and references are in the white paper.
  • Laboratory tests. DNA-damage, telomere and methylation tests are research tools for comparing groups [40,59]. This initiative does not offer or recommend them for individuals.
  • Air data. Readings come from the OpenAQ API (version 3) and are updated once a day by an automated workflow.
    • Providers and licences: loading…
    • Our own rules, not part of the AQI scale:
      • A value is shown only if at least 18 of the 24 hourly readings exist.
      • Averages are truncated to one decimal place.
      • Data older than 24 hours are hidden.
    • Sensors. All 15 locations are AirGradient sensors, chosen on 13 November 2026. Their accuracy has not been checked against reference instruments.
  • Limitations.
    • Most human studies estimate exposure from home address or region.
    • Little of the genetic research comes from Pakistan.
    • No biological measurements were made at these locations.
