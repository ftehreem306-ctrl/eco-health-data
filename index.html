What PM2.5 does in the body

PM2.5 means fine inhalable particles with diameters generally 2.5 µm and smaller [1], measured as aerodynamic diameter [2]. The US EPA gives an average human hair as about 70 µm, about 30 times larger than the largest fine particle [1].

The US EPA states that some particles under 10 µm can get deep into the lungs and some may get into the bloodstream, and identifies particles under 2.5 µm as the greatest risk to health [1]. Direct human evidence is limited: in one small study, engineered ultrafine gold particles inhaled by volunteers were found in blood and urine, and were still present three months later. These were test particles, not outdoor PM2.5 [14].

Air pollution is thought to cause oxidative stress and inflammation, which give rise to chronic disease [13].

  • Death, long-term exposure. In cohort studies pooled for WHO, each 10 µg/m³ more long-term PM2.5 was associated with an 8% higher risk of death from natural causes (relative risk 1.08) [15]. An update of 106 studies found similar results [16].
  • Death, short-term exposure. Each 10 µg/m³ rise in 24-hour PM2.5 was associated with about 0.65% more deaths from all causes [17].
  • Cancer. IARC classifies outdoor air pollution and the particulate matter in it as carcinogenic to humans (Group 1), with sufficient evidence for lung cancer [18,19].
  • Pregnancy. A meta-analysis found small associations with preterm birth and low birth weight in full-term babies, with considerable variation between studies [20].
  • Epigenetics. Human studies of PM2.5 and DNA methylation are inconsistent and poorly replicated, and none shows that these changes cause disease [21–23].

These findings describe what exposure is associated with in the published literature. No health, clinical or epigenetic effects have been measured at the 15 locations on this page.

Daily defence playbook

Each item has an evidence label. Moderate means a few randomised trials or a meta-analysis with varied results. Limited means laboratory tests only, small studies, or indirect evidence. The trials measured short-term outcomes such as blood pressure, not heart attacks or deaths [33].

Do

Run a HEPA air cleaner sized for the room

Moderate In 17 trials, real filtration lowered indoor PM2.5 and systolic blood pressure compared with sham filtration [28]. A one-year trial in older adults also found lower indoor PM2.5 [29]. Choose a clean air delivery rate that suits the room size [30].

If you wear a mask, make sure it fits

Moderate In trials in Beijing, wearing a facemask improved short-term blood pressure and heart measures [32,33]. Fit matters: when masks were worn, leakage ranged from 3% to 68% [31].

Check the category and adjust outdoor exertion

Moderate Follow the actions in the readings table [12,34]. Expert guidance grades checking forecasts, staying indoors, air cleaners and masks by strength of evidence [35], and advises exercising away from pollution sources [30].

Keep windows closed during pollution peaks

Limited In one study, indoor PM2.5, blood pressure and heart rate were higher with windows open than closed [36].

A do-it-yourself box-fan filter is an option

Limited A box fan with five MERV-13 filters cleaned air effectively in a laboratory test [37]. No health studies were found.

Don't

Don't burn household waste

Moderate Burning municipal waste contributes about 7% of population-weighted PM2.5 in Punjab [8]. No study was found on the personal health benefit of stopping.

Don't rely on supplements for protection

Limited A review graded the evidence for every diet and supplement category as C, and found the evidence for omega-3 and N-acetylcysteine mostly observational [38].

The bigger picture

Burden in Pakistan

  • Modelling attributes more than 200,000 deaths in Pakistan in 2023 to air pollution [13]. An earlier modelling round gave 256,000 for 2021 [24]. These come from different modelling rounds, so they are not a trend.
  • A satellite-based estimate puts Pakistan's average PM2.5 in 2023 at about 39 µg/m³ [6]. The WHO annual guideline is 5 µg/m³ [3].
  • The World Bank reports Punjab's population-weighted annual average at about 52 µg/m³, and annual averages of 110–130 µg/m³ in central Lahore [8].
  • Punjab's largest modelled sources are natural sources (about 25%), household cooking with solid fuels (23%), transport (16%) and industry including brick kilns (16%) [8].
  • World Bank estimates of the economic cost range from 2.5–6.5% of GDP (2016) to 9% of GDP (2019), using different methods [26,27,8].
  • Official sources disagree on Pakistan's annual PM2.5 standard: one gives 15 µg/m³ and another 35 µg/m³ [4–7].

What policy has done elsewhere

  • London's Ultra Low Emission Zone. Evaluations disagree. The city estimated a 37% fall in roadside nitrogen dioxide [39]. An independent analysis found under 3%, with no significant effect on PM2.5 [40]. A 2025 study found a 28.8% fall in NOx at central London traffic sites after the first phase [41].
  • China's 2013 Action Plan. Population-weighted PM2.5 fell from 61.8 to 42.0 µg/m³ between 2013 and 2017 [42]. A clean-heating programme in northern China was followed by lower PM2.5 and fewer heart attacks in covered areas [13].
  • Pakistan. The 2023 National Clean Air Policy lists measures such as cleaner fuel standards and preventing crop-residue burning [7]. No published evaluation of it was found.

Methods and data

  • Source. Readings come from the OpenAQ API (version 3). A script runs once a day on GitHub Actions and saves the results to data/pm25.json, which this page reads.
  • Providers and licences: loading…
  • Value shown. The mean of hourly PM2.5 averages over the 24 hours before the update, in µg/m³. The AQI breakpoints are defined for 24-hour averages [11].
  • This project's own rules.
    • A value is shown only if at least 18 of the 24 hourly readings exist.
    • The average is truncated to one decimal place before a category is assigned.
    • Data more than 24 hours old are hidden and shown as "no recent data".
    These rules are not part of the AQI scale.
  • Choice of locations. The 15 locations were chosen on 13 November 2026 from all PM2.5 locations OpenAQ lists for Pakistan. We chose big cities, sensors that had reported recently, and one sensor network for consistency. At that time, the five Pakistani locations that OpenAQ flags as monitors had not reported since February 2026 or earlier, so none could be used.
  • Sensors. All 15 locations are AirGradient sensors, which OpenAQ does not flag as monitors. This project has not checked their accuracy against reference instruments.
  • Limitations.
    • One sensor cannot represent a whole city.
    • No health outcomes were measured at these locations.
    • The health and burden figures are from published studies and models, not from these readings.
    • The full evidence and its limits are in the white paper.
