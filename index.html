What the air in Pakistan does to our DNA, and what we can do about it

Eco-Health Action Hub turns research on fine particle pollution (PM2.5), genes, epigenetics and cancer into plain language for families. Every finding is labelled as known, suspected or unknown.

    Three facts to start with

    Group 1WHO's cancer agency classifies outdoor air pollution and its particles as causing cancer in humans, with sufficient evidence for lung cancer. [12,13]
    About 195,000cases of one common type of lung cancer (adenocarcinoma) worldwide in 2022 were linked to outdoor particle pollution. [3]
    52 µg/m³is the average PM2.5 exposure across Punjab's population, more than ten times the WHO annual guideline of 5 µg/m³. [10,11]

    How sure is the science?

    We sort every finding by how strong the evidence is, so you can tell a proven risk from an open question.

    Known

    • Polluted air causes lung cancer. [12,13]
    • Long-term exposure raises lung-cancer risk, including in people who have never smoked. [2,3]
    • Mothers who breathe more polluted air are more likely to have babies born early or small. [4,5]
    • Soot particles have been found in placentas, cord blood and the organs of unborn babies. [6,7]

    Suspected

    • Particles help lung cells that are already faulty grow into tumours (shown in mice). [15]
    • Heavily exposed workers show more DNA damage in blood tests. [17,19]
    • Pollution changes the chemical "tags" (methylation) on DNA in adults and newborns. [23,24]
    • Some inherited gene types make people more sensitive. [33,34]

    Unknown

    • What the particles found in the placenta do to the baby. [7]
    • Whether pollution speeds up the body's biological clock: pooled studies could not confirm it. [28]
    • Whether grandchildren are affected: our review found no source.
    • Whether cleaner air reverses DNA changes: our review found no source.
