Your DNA and the air

Researchers look for signs of pollution in DNA itself: breaks, chemical tags and markers of ageing. Most of these findings are suspected, not proven.

Damage to DNA

2 findings

Suspected Breaks in DNA

The comet assay is a laboratory test that measures breaks in DNA. People exposed to smoke from traffic, or from wood, dung or crop-waste fires, showed more DNA breaks in their blood cells than comparison groups. The authors note possible confounding and measurement bias. [17]

People exposed to particles also showed more micronuclei, small broken pieces of chromosomes. [18]

Suspected Studies from Pakistan

In Karak, traffic conductors had an average comet tail length of 28.69 µm against 8.62 µm in comparison people, and coal miners 30.16 µm against 9.82 µm. [19] Among traffic police wardens, 28% of cells were damaged against 3.6% in comparison people; the authors linked this to cadmium and zinc. [20] In laboratory tests, PM2.5 collected in Lahore increased markers of DNA and chromosome damage in cells. [21]

These studies are small and mostly cover workers, so they should not be applied to whole cities.

Chemical tags and biological age

3 findings

Suspected DNA methylation

The best-studied epigenetic change is DNA methylation, the chemical "tags" attached to DNA. [23,24] In a small trial, breathing PM2.5 for two hours changed DNA methylation measured in blood. Whether this harms health is unknown. [23] Reviews find inconsistent links between PM2.5 and overall DNA methylation, and most studies came from countries with low pollution levels. [25,26,27]

Unknown "Epigenetic clocks"

These estimate the body's biological age from methylation patterns, and the studies disagree. [28]

Suspected Chromosome "end caps"

Telomeres are the "end caps" on chromosomes, a sign linked to ageing. In 433,535 UK adults, air pollution was associated with telomere length, and inherited genetic risk added to the effect. [22] In a small experiment, two hours of breathing PM2.5 lowered mitochondrial DNA content by 11.1%. [23]

Genes that change sensitivity

1 finding

Suspected GST gene types

Some people inherit inactive ("null") versions of genes called GSTM1 and GSTT1. In one review, 15 of 22 studies found that these genes changed the link between indoor air pollution and asthma or lung function. [33] A 2026 review of 48 studies found 89 gene variants, in 53 genes, that significantly changed the effects of outdoor pollution on breathing health. [34] A 2023 review concluded that the role of the GSTM1-null type is not yet clear. [35]
