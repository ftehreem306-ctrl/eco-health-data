Air and cancer

Breathing polluted air for years raises the chance of lung cancer, including for people who have never smoked. Here is what the research shows, and what it does not.

What the labels mean

In one minute

  • KnownOutdoor air pollution and its particles cause lung cancer in humans. [12,13]
  • KnownLung-cancer risk rises about 9% for each 10 µg/m³ more PM2.5 over the long term. [2]
  • SuspectedParticles may help lung cells that already carry mutations grow into tumours. [15]
  • UnknownWhether PM2.5 causes breast, bladder or liver cancer, or childhood leukaemia.

The risk

2 findings

Known Lung cancer

The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), WHO's cancer agency, classifies outdoor air pollution and the particles in it as causing cancer in humans (Group 1). It found sufficient evidence for lung cancer. [12,13]

A meta-analysis found that lung-cancer risk rose by about 9% for each 10 µg/m³ of long-term PM2.5 exposure (relative risk 1.09). [2]

Suspected People who have never smoked

The estimate for never-smokers was 1.18, with a lower limit of 1.00, so this evidence is moderate rather than strong. [2]

IARC researchers estimate that about 195,000 cases of adenocarcinoma, one common type of lung cancer, were linked to outdoor particle pollution worldwide in 2022: 114,486 in men and 80,378 in women. [3] IARC describes "accumulating evidence of a causal link". [14]

How it might happen

2 findings

Suspected Faulty cells that already exist

In normal-looking lung tissue, scientists found mutations in the cancer-driver gene EGFR in 18% of samples and in KRAS in 53%. Many healthy people already carry a few faulty cells. [15]

In mice, pollution particles drew in immune cells that released a signal called IL-1β. This inflammation helped already-mutated lung cells grow into early tumours. In four human cohorts covering 32,957 cases, higher PM2.5 levels were associated with more EGFR-driven lung cancer. [15]

Suspected What tumour genomes show

The Sherlock-Lung study sequenced the tumours of 871 never-smokers. Tumours from regions with higher PM2.5 carried more mutations, more TP53 mutations and shorter telomeres. Exposure was estimated by region, so the study cannot show what caused any one person's cancer. [16]

What we don't know

1 finding

Unknown Other cancers

Our review found no source strong enough to say whether PM2.5 causes breast, bladder or liver cancer, or childhood leukaemia. We make no claim about them.
